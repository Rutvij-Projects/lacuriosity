Start with four hundred years of dark water. The North Atlantic turns over slowly. Ice sheets advance and retreat along Greenland's coast. Ships cross it under sail, then under steam, then under diesel. Whaling fleets come and go. Two world wars pass overhead. Through all of it, deep in cold Arctic water, something enormous keeps swimming at the pace of a slow walk.

Now zoom in on one animal: a female Greenland shark, about five metres long and soft-bodied, hauled up dead in a fisheries survey net. Fishermen had pulled her kind out of these waters for centuries. Nobody had ever been able to say how old one was.

## A fish with no clock

Most fish keep a diary in their bones. Ear stones and vertebrae lay down growth rings, like a tree. Count the rings, and you have the age.

The Greenland shark refuses to cooperate. Its skeleton is soft cartilage with no readable rings. The marine biologist Steven Campana, at the University of Iceland, told *Science News* that aging these sharks had "stymied all solution attempts". It was, he said, almost unbelievable that nobody knew whether the species lived to 20 years or to 1,000.

So a team led by Julius Nielsen, then a PhD student at the University of Copenhagen, went looking for a clock somewhere else. They found one in the eye.

## The clock in the eye

The lens of a vertebrate eye is built from clear proteins. Its very centre, the nucleus, forms before birth and then never changes. No new material arrives. No old material leaves. That small core is a time capsule, sealed the year the animal was born.

Nielsen's team needed a way to read it. They used radiocarbon, a slightly radioactive form of carbon that every living thing absorbs from its food.

Here humans had left a marker without meaning to. Nuclear bomb tests in the 1950s flooded the atmosphere with extra radiocarbon. That surge, called the bomb pulse, reached the sharks' corner of the North Atlantic in the 1960s. Any shark born after it arrived carries the spike in the centre of its lens. Any shark born before does not.

The team examined the eye lenses of 28 female Greenland sharks, all caught by accident during fisheries surveys. Only three, the smallest, showed the bomb signal. That told the scientists roughly how big a shark is when it is born into the bomb era. With that as a fixed point, plus the radiocarbon levels and the size of the others, they could estimate the ages of the larger sharks.

> The biggest one, the five-metre female, came out at about 392 years old.

## How sure can anyone be?

Not very, and the scientists say so plainly. The estimate carries a margin of about 120 years either way. The study, published in *Science* in August 2016, gives a range of roughly 272 to 512 years for that largest shark. Even the low end beats the bowhead whale, the previous vertebrate record holder, estimated at 211 years.

Campana, who was not part of the study, remained cautious about the headline number. Other sharks typically live 10 to 80 years, he pointed out. He was willing to accept that this one grows for more than a century. Four centuries was harder to swallow.

That debate is healthy. But the core finding has held up: the Greenland shark lives far longer than anyone had suspected. The same analysis suggested females do not reach sexual maturity until they are about 156 years old. A shark has to outlive every person who was alive on the day it was born before it can start a family of its own.

## Life in slow motion

How does an animal last that long? Part of the answer is temperature and pace.

In 2012, researchers led by Yuuki Watanabe fixed data loggers to six free-swimming Greenland sharks. The sharks cruised at about 0.34 metres per second, around 1.2 kilometres per hour. Their tails beat once every six or seven seconds. Even at full burst, they managed only about 0.74 metres per second. For their size, those were the lowest numbers recorded for any fish. (A shark research group has since disputed the "slowest fish" title, so that label is contested.)

Cold water slows chemistry. Slow chemistry slows growth, metabolism and, perhaps, ageing. More recently, the first full Greenland shark genomes have pointed to expanded families of genes linked to DNA repair and immune defence. The researchers behind that work stress there is no single "long life" gene. The answer seems to be many small adjustments, stacked over a very long time.

## Zoom back out

Pull back from the one shark, and the picture widens again.

If the central estimate is right, she was born in the early 1600s. Galileo was still alive. The Mayflower had only just crossed the same ocean. She grew through the age of sail, the age of steam and the age of the atom. The atom left its fingerprint in her younger cousins' eyes. Her own eyes held a cleaner record from before all of it.

Greenland sharks are now listed as Near Threatened by the IUCN. They were once hunted in large numbers for their liver oil. Today they die mostly as bycatch in trawls, nets and fish traps. An animal that takes over a century to breed cannot replace losses quickly. A large shark killed by accident this year may have been swimming since before the steam engine.

There is something steadying in that. The ocean still holds animals that measure time in centuries, quietly cruising through the cold while the rest of us rush. Scientists can now read the dates in their eyes. The next question is whether we can give them the time they need.

**Bjarnarhöfn Shark Museum, north side of the Snæfellsnes peninsula, west Iceland.** The farm at Bjarnarhöfn has caught and cured Greenland shark for centuries, and its small museum explains how. Because the raw meat is toxic, it is fermented and dried for months to make *hákarl*, Iceland's famously pungent national dish. Visitors can see the drying house and, if they dare, taste a piece.
