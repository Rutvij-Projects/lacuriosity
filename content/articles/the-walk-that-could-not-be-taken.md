What becomes possible if everyone understood that some questions are not about size at all?

Not how far. Not how heavy. Not how many metres from this door to that one. Only what touches what. Consider how much of your life runs on that kind of question: which roads join which towns, which wires join which machines, which friends know which friends. Perhaps the oddest thing about the mathematics behind all of it is where it began. It began with a walk that nobody could take.

## A city on a fork in the river

Picture the city of Königsberg in the 1700s. The River Pregel runs through it and splits around two large islands, Kneiphof and Lomse. The water carves the city into four pieces of land: the two islands, and the two mainland banks, Altstadt and Vorstadt. Seven bridges stitch those four pieces together.

Stand on one of those bridges. Wood and stone underfoot, the slow brown water sliding beneath, a church roof on the island ahead. Now imagine a simple wish. Could you stroll through the city and cross every one of the seven bridges exactly once? No skipping a bridge. No crossing one twice.

People tried. Nobody managed it. But nobody could say why.

## A letter from Danzig

The question reached Leonhard Euler, then working at the Academy of Sciences in St. Petersburg, by post. It came from Carl Gottlieb Ehler, mayor of the city of Danzig, who had been passing along mathematical questions from his friend Heinrich Kühn, a local mathematician. In March 1736, Ehler asked Euler for a solution to "the problem of the seven Königsberg bridges together with a proof." He suggested it would be a fine example of what he called the calculus of position.

Euler's reply, in April 1736, sounds almost grumpy. He told Ehler that this kind of solution "bears little relationship to mathematics," because it rested on reason alone. He even wondered aloud why anyone would expect a mathematician, rather than anyone else, to find it.

And yet he could not leave it alone. Something in the puzzle had caught him.

> The greatest mathematician of his century looked at seven bridges and said this is barely mathematics. Then he solved it, and changed what mathematics could be.

## Throwing the map away

Here is the move that makes this story worth telling. Euler realised that almost everything about the map did not matter.

The length of each bridge? Irrelevant. The shape of the islands, the width of the river, the streets, the distances? All irrelevant. The only thing that mattered was which pieces of land were joined, and by how many bridges.

So he stripped the city down to its bones. Four pieces of land. Seven connections. Nothing else.

Then he noticed something quietly powerful. Every time you walk onto a piece of land by a bridge, you must walk off it by another bridge. Arrivals and departures come in pairs. That means any piece of land you pass through, rather than start or finish on, must be touched by an even number of bridges.

Only two places escape that rule: the spot where your walk begins and the spot where it ends. So at most two pieces of land may have an odd number of bridges.

Now count Königsberg. One piece of land is touched by five bridges. Each of the other three is touched by three. All four are odd. The walk is impossible, and not because nobody was clever enough. It is impossible the way a square circle is impossible. Euler had not found a route. He had proved that no route could exist.

## A new kind of geometry

Euler presented his work to the St. Petersburg Academy on 26 August 1735, and it was published in 1741 under a Latin title that translates as *The solution of a problem relating to the geometry of position*.

That phrase, geometry of position, has its own history. The philosopher and mathematician Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz had given the name *geometria situs* to a kind of geometry he hoped someone would build: one about position rather than measurement. Euler admitted to Ehler that he did not really know what this new discipline involved. Consider the gentle irony. The man who said he did not understand the field had just written one of its first real results.

Euler also stated the general rule, for any arrangement of land and bridges. Much later, the mathematician Carl Hierholzer proved the other half: that when the odd-bridge condition is met, a complete walk really can be found.

Euler drew no dots and lines. Those came later. But today his paper is widely regarded as the first step in graph theory, the study of things (dots) and connections (lines). That is the language behind subway maps, computer networks, and the web of who-follows-whom.

## What the city looks like now

History has redrawn the puzzle. Königsberg is now Kaliningrad, in Russia. Two of the seven original bridges did not survive the bombing of the city in World War II. Two more were later demolished and replaced by a highway. Of the three that remain, only two date from Euler's time.

The strange result is that, according to one careful accounting, a walk crossing each of the bridges at the old sites exactly once is now possible. You just have to begin on one island and end on the other. War and city planning did what no stroller could. They changed the count.

Perhaps that is the real lesson in the stone and water. The answer was never in the walking. It was in the counting, and anyone who understands the counting can look at a map of anything, from a city to a circuit board, and know at a glance whether the journey is possible.

## Look Closer

This week, read Euler's own paper. It is short, about a dozen pages in the original, and surprisingly friendly; he walks you through his reasoning the way a teacher would. A complete English translation appears on pages 3 to 8 of *Graph Theory 1736–1936* by Norman Biggs, E. Keith Lloyd and Robin Wilson, which most university libraries hold. For a free, slightly trimmed version, the University of Minnesota hosts a translated excerpt as a PDF (linked below). Read it with a pencil, and try drawing your own town's bridges as dots and lines.
