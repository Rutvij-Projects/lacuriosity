Glass is fragile. Everyone knows that. Drop a wine glass and you reach for the broom.

So here is a piece of glass that breaks the rule. Lay its round head on an anvil and hit it with a hammer. Nothing. Squeeze it in a press with roughly the weight of a car pressing down on it. Still nothing.

Now take its thin glass tail between your fingers and give it a gentle bend. The whole thing explodes into powder with a sharp pop, faster than you can blink.

It is called a Prince Rupert's drop, and for about 400 years it has been embarrassing very clever people.

## A toy for kings

Nobody knows exactly who made the first one. Written accounts of the drops turn up in Mecklenburg, in northern Germany, as early as 1625, and glassmakers in the Netherlands were producing them too. People called them "Dutch tears" or "Prussian tears."

The recipe is simple and a little dangerous. A glassmaker lets a red-hot blob of molten glass fall into a bucket of cold water. It hisses and freezes into a tadpole shape: a fat round head, usually 5 to 15 millimetres across, trailing a tail thinner than a strand of spaghetti.

In 1660, Prince Rupert of the Rhine, a German-born prince, brought some of these curiosities to London. He did not invent them, but his name stuck. King Charles II was charmed. In March 1661 he sent five "little glass bubbles" to the brand-new Royal Society and, in effect, asked the smartest people in England a question: how does this work?

That August, Sir Robert Moray, one of the Society's founders, reported back on how the drops were made and how they behaved. Then the scientist Robert Hooke gave them a whole chapter of *Micrographia*, his famous 1665 book about the tiny world seen through a microscope.

## Hooke's brilliant guess

Hooke noticed something important. When the drop hits the water, the outside cools almost instantly and goes stiff. The inside is still glowing hot and soft.

As the inside slowly cools, it tries to shrink. But it is trapped inside a hard shell that has already set. So the inside pulls inward on the shell, and the shell gets squeezed tight. Hooke described this locked-in "springy tension" holding the drop in balance, much like the keystone of an arch.

That guess turned out to be right. Proving it, with numbers, took another three and a half centuries.

> The strongest part of the drop is not the glass. It is the squeeze the glass is under.

## Where glass actually fails

Here is what most of us get wrong. Glass is not weak because it is soft. It is weak because it is covered in tiny invisible scratches.

When you pull on glass, those scratches pull open. A crack starts at one, races through, and the glass snaps. Engineers call this tensile stress, the stretching kind. One of the researchers who later solved the drop, K. Viswanathan, compared it to tearing a sheet of paper in half. The mathematics of how cracks grow like this was not worked out until A. A. Griffith in 1920.

But if the surface of the glass is already being squeezed hard, those scratches are pressed shut. A crack cannot open. Your hammer has to beat the squeeze first before it can do any damage at all.

Here is a kitchen-scale way to picture it. Line up a row of books on a shelf and press both ends together firmly with your hands. Now try to pull one book out of the middle. You can't, because everything is jammed tight. Stop pushing, and the book slides right out. The drop's head is a shelf of books that never stops being pushed.

## Measuring the squeeze

In 2016 and 2017, a team from Purdue University in Indiana, the University of Cambridge and Tallinn University of Technology in Estonia finally put a number on it.

They used a trick called photoelasticity. Glass under stress bends light differently from relaxed glass. So the team, including the Estonian stress expert Hillar Aben, suspended a drop in a clear liquid, shone red LED light through it, and measured how the light was twisted. Stressed glass glows with rainbow bands through polarizing filters, and the team used maths similar to a medical CT scan to rebuild the stress inside the whole drop.

The results, published in *Applied Physics Letters*:

- The surface of the head is squeezed with a force of 400 to 700 megapascals.
- At the top of that range, that is nearly 7,000 times the pressure of the air around you.
- The squeezed skin is thin, only about 10% of the head's diameter.
- The head survived being crushed between tungsten carbide plates with a load of 15,000 newtons, roughly the weight of a car.

The surprise was that it is the squeeze on the outside, not the tension inside, that makes the head so tough. "The work has fully explained why the head of a drop is so strong," said M. M. Chaudhri of Cambridge.

## The weak spot

So why does the tail destroy everything?

Because deep inside the drop, the story flips. The core is still being stretched, pulling against the squeezed shell. All that stored energy is just waiting for a way in.

The tail is so thin that a crack started there does not have far to go. Bend it, and the crack slips through into the stretched core. Once it gets there, the stored tension rips it forward.

In 1994, S. Chandrasekar of Purdue and Chaudhri filmed this with a high-speed camera running at nearly a million frames per second. They saw cracks racing from the tail toward the head at more than 4,000 miles per hour, splitting again and again until the whole drop was dust. As Chandrasekar put it, the drop shatters "within a few microseconds."

## From curiosity to your car window

The idea locked inside a 17th-century party trick is now all around you.

In 1874, a French inventor named François Barthélemy Alfred Royer de la Bastie patented a way to toughen glass by heating it and cooling it fast. The drops probably helped inspire it. Today, the same trick of a squeezed surface over a stretched core is used in toughened glass for car side windows, and a similar process helps make shatter-resistant phone screens.

That is why a car's side window crumbles into little rounded cubes when it finally breaks, instead of long dangerous shards. It is, in a sense, a giant flat Prince Rupert's drop. "There was a time lag of more than 200 years," Chandrasekar said of the gap between the drops and the first patents.

Some questions take centuries. That is not a failure. It is how wonder works.

## Try This At Home

Please don't drop molten glass into water. But you can see hidden stress in your own home, the same way the scientists did.

You need two things: a laptop or computer screen showing a plain white page, and a piece of clear, hard plastic, like a plastic fork, a cheap ruler, a CD case, or a clear food container lid. If you have polarized sunglasses, grab them too.

Most LCD screens give off polarized light. Hold the clear plastic in front of the white screen and look at it through the sunglasses. Turn the sunglasses slowly until the screen goes dark. Suddenly the plastic lights up with bands of rainbow colour.

Those colours are frozen stress, trapped in the plastic when it was moulded and cooled. Now gently bend a plastic fork while you watch. The rainbow shifts and crowds together where the strain is greatest.

You are doing photoelasticity, the same science that finally cracked Prince Rupert's drops. What else around you is quietly holding itself together under pressure?
