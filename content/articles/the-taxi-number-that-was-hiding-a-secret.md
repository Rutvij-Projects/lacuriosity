What becomes possible if everyone understood that there is no such thing as a boring number?

Consider the numbers you pass every day without a glance. The bus route. The flat on the third floor. The last four digits of a phone number. Perhaps each one is a closed door, and all it takes is the right person to knock. This is the story of one such door, a number painted on the side of a London taxi, and the man who knew at once what was behind it.

## A visit to Putney

Srinivasa Ramanujan was born in 1887 in a small village in southern India, roughly 400 kilometres from Madras, now Chennai. He had almost no formal training in advanced mathematics. What he had was a notebook habit and an astonishing feel for how numbers fit together. In 1914 he arrived at Cambridge to work with G. H. Hardy, one of the finest mathematicians in England.

The partnership was extraordinary. Hardy later called it "the one romantic incident in my life."

But England was hard on Ramanujan. The cold climate and wartime rationing wore him down, and by 1918 he was seriously ill. He was being cared for in Putney, on the edge of London, when Hardy came to see him.

Hardy told the story himself, years later, in his 1940 book *Ramanujan*. He had ridden over in taxi cab No. 1729. Making conversation, perhaps a little awkwardly, he remarked that the number seemed "rather a dull one."

Ramanujan, ill in bed, did not hesitate. No, he said, it was a very interesting number. It was the smallest number that can be written as the sum of two cubes in two different ways.

> A sick man in a bed in Putney heard four digits and saw straight through them to their skeleton.

## Two ways to build 1729

Here is what Ramanujan saw. A cube is a number multiplied by itself three times. Two cubed is 2 × 2 × 2, which is 8. Picture building blocks: a cube of side 10 contains 1,000 small blocks.

Now take two piles of blocks.

- A cube of side 1 (1 block) and a cube of side 12 (1,728 blocks). Together: 1,729.
- A cube of side 9 (729 blocks) and a cube of side 10 (1,000 blocks). Together: 1,729.

Two completely different pairs of cubes, the same total. Try any smaller number and you will not find two such pairs of positive whole cubes. 1729 is the first. Mathematicians now call numbers like this taxicab numbers, in honour of that ride.

Was Ramanujan calculating on the spot? Almost certainly not. The number already appeared in his notebooks, written down years before Hardy's visit. And he was not even the first to notice it. The French mathematician Bernard Frénicle de Bessy had recorded the same fact in 1657. Ramanujan simply recognised an old friend.

1729 has other quiet talents, too. It equals 7 × 13 × 19. Its digits add up to 19, and 19 × 91 is 1729. It belongs to a rare family called Carmichael numbers, which can fool certain quick tests for primes into thinking they are prime when they are not. A dull number, it turns out, had been wearing several disguises at once.

## The box at the bottom of the archive

For almost a century, the taxi story was told as a charming anecdote: proof that Ramanujan knew numbers the way you know the faces of your friends. Then the story got deeper.

Ramanujan returned to India in 1919, still weak, and died the following year. He was only 32. His surviving work is scattered: three notebooks kept at the University of Madras, and one box of papers kept at Trinity College, Cambridge.

In 2013 the number theorist Ken Ono, of Emory University, was in England visiting fellow mathematicians Andrew Granville and John Coates. Ono and Granville went to look through the Ramanujan box at Trinity's Wren Library. "We were sitting right next to the librarian's desk, flipping page by page through the Ramanujan box," Ono recalled. Then they reached a page with two representations of 1729 on it.

They started laughing. Ono said they were floored.

## So close to impossible

To see why, you need one more piece of history. In the 1600s, Pierre de Fermat claimed that certain equations have no whole-number solutions at all. The simplest case says you can never add two cubes and get a third cube. No matter how hard you search, x³ + y³ will never exactly equal z³. That claim, part of what became known as Fermat's Last Theorem, took over 350 years to prove in full, finally falling to Andrew Wiles in the 1990s.

Now look at 1729 again. It is 9³ + 10³. It is also 12³ + 1. Two cubes add up to a third cube, plus one. It is a near miss: as close as you can get to breaking Fermat's rule without breaking it.

What Ono and Granville found on that page was not a single near miss. Ramanujan had written down a method that produces infinitely many of them, an endless family of cube sums that land just one away from a perfect cube. "We were shocked by that," Ono said.

Ono and his graduate student Sarah Trebat-Leder then studied the page more closely, and published their findings in 2015. Their conclusion was startling. To produce those near misses, Ramanujan had been working with a kind of geometric object that mathematicians now call a K3 surface. These shapes were only formally identified and named in the 1950s, by the mathematician André Weil, who chose the name to honour three mathematicians, Kummer, Kähler and Kodaira, and the mountain K2. K3 surfaces are now a major subject in modern geometry.

Ramanujan seems to have been working with one, scribbled in a box of papers, more than 30 years before anyone gave it a name.

"It's as though he left a magic key for the mathematicians of the future," Ono said.

## The number on the door

Perhaps the lesson is not that Ramanujan was a genius, though he was. It is that the dullness Hardy saw was never in the number. It was in the looking.

Consider the taxi again. A tired man climbs out, glances at the plate, and sees nothing. His friend, too ill to stand, hears the same four digits and sees two towers of blocks, a near miss with a 300-year-old puzzle, and a shape the world would not name for another generation. Same number. Different eyes.

The next time a number crosses your path, a bus, a receipt, a page number, knock on the door. Factor it. Add its digits. Ask whether it can be built in two ways. Most of the time you will find something small. Once in a while, you might find a key.

## Look Closer

This week, read *The Man Who Knew Infinity* by Robert Kanigel, the 1991 biography of Ramanujan. It tells the whole story, from the village in Tamil Nadu to Cambridge and back, including the taxi ride. It is widely available in paperback, as an ebook and through most public libraries. If you would rather watch than read, the 2015 film of the same name, with Dev Patel as Ramanujan and Jeremy Irons as Hardy, is based on the book.
