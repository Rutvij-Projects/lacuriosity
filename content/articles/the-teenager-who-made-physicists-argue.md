Put two identical cups of water in a freezer. One is cold. The other has just been boiled. Every rule you learned says the cold one freezes first, because it has less heat to lose and a shorter way to go.

In 1963, a student in Tanzania said that rule sometimes fails. Physicists have been arguing about him ever since.

## The cookery class

Erasto Mpemba was studying at Magamba Secondary School in what was then Tanganyika. In a cookery class, he was making ice cream, and he put his mixture into the freezer while it was still hot. It froze before a cooler mixture did.

He never let the question go. Later, at another school, a visiting physicist named Denis Osborne gave a lecture, and Mpemba put the question to him directly. Take two equal containers of water, one at 35 °C and one at 100 °C. Why does the hot one freeze first?

Osborne assumed the boy was mistaken. Then he tried it in his own laboratory, and found he could confirm it. In 1969 the two of them published a paper together.

> A rule that works a thousand times is still just a rule. One honest exception is enough to make it interesting.

## An old suspicion

Mpemba was not the first to notice. Aristotle wrote that warming water beforehand helps it freeze quickly. Francis Bacon and René Descartes both made similar remarks. In 1775, the Scottish scientist Joseph Black found that water that had been boiled froze faster than water that had not.

So the puzzle is ancient. What Mpemba added was a question nobody could brush aside.

## The suspects

Why would hot water win? Osborne and Mpemba checked the obvious answers. Hot water loses some volume to evaporation, which leaves less water to freeze. Hot water also holds less dissolved air. In their setup, neither explained it.

A better clue came from supercooling. Water can drop well below 0 °C and still stay liquid, waiting for a speck or a crystal to start the freeze. In 1995, David Auerbach studied this in glass beakers. Water that started cooler tended to supercool further before it froze, which handed some hotter samples a head start. The timing also varied wildly from one run to the next.

That randomness hints at the real problem. The question is not only why hot water might win. It is what "winning" means.

## Where is the finish line?

Think of a kitchen race. Does the water "freeze" when the first skin of ice appears on top? When it reaches 0 °C? When the whole cup is a solid block? Each finish line gives a different winner, and experiments have used all of them.

In 2016, Henry Burridge and Paul Linden published a careful study in Scientific Reports. They timed how long water took to reach 0 °C, and found no hot-water advantage at all. Whatever they tried, they could not see anything like the Mpemba effect. Critics replied that reaching 0 °C is not freezing, and that the interesting action happens at the moment ice first forms.

So both camps can be right about different finish lines. The effect appears in some setups, vanishes in others, and depends on the container, the cooling method and the definition.

## The bead that settled part of it

In 2020, Avinash Kumar and John Bechhoefer at Simon Fraser University took a cleaner approach. Water is messy. So they trapped a tiny glass bead in a beaker of water and controlled the forces on it, letting the surrounding molecules jostle it randomly. In that system, they could produce the effect on demand. A hotter starting state could settle into the cold state faster than a cooler one, if the system was set up in the right way.

That did not prove that your cup of water does it. It did show that nothing in physics forbids it. A system can take a shortcut through its own states, and a hotter start can sometimes land on the shortcut.

The same group later found the mirror image: under the right conditions, a cold particle can warm up faster than a warmer one.

## Why it matters

The Mpemba effect is a small experiment with a big lesson. A strange result does not have to be true everywhere to be real somewhere. Mpemba's question pushed physicists to define their terms, and the best physics often begins when someone insists on that.

It also reminds us how much of the world sits just outside our rules of thumb. The next time something behaves oddly, be as stubborn as a student with a tray of ice cream.

## Try This At Home

You can run Mpemba's experiment, with care. Pour 70 ml of water into each of two identical small cups. Heat one to about 90 °C and leave the other at room temperature. Be careful: hot water scalds. Set both on a sheet of polystyrene foam, side by side in the freezer, and check them every few minutes. Decide first what counts as "frozen".

Run it five times. Most likely, the cold cup will win some rounds and the hot cup will win others. That scatter is exactly what the scientists are arguing about.
