Sunday, November 18, 1883. Noon arrives in an American city. The clocks strike twelve.

Then, a few minutes later, they strike twelve again.

Nobody voted for this. No president signed it. No court approved it. The second noon was decided five weeks earlier, in a hotel in Chicago, by the men who ran the railroads. Their decision still sits on your wrist, your phone and your microwave today.

## A country of a thousand noons

Before 1883, time was local. Noon was the moment the sun stood highest over *your* town. Walk a few kilometres east or west and noon came at a slightly different moment. Every town set its clocks by its own sky. By one count, more than 8,000 American towns kept their own local time.

For farmers, that was fine. For a railroad, it was chaos.

A train is a schedule on wheels. If it leaves one city at 10:00 and reaches the next at 11:30, someone has to say *whose* 10:00 and *whose* 11:30. Each railroad picked its own answer, usually the time of its headquarters city or a major station. By the early 1880s, American railways ran on about 50 different railroad times. Two railroads serving the same city could disagree about what time it was.

## The schoolteacher who saw it first

The fix was not invented by a railroad man.

Around 1870, Charles Ferdinand Dowd, who ran Temple Grove Seminary, a school for girls in Saratoga Springs, New York, published a plan for national railway time. He split the country into bands, each one hour apart. He was not a railroad man, but he took his plan to the people who ran the railroads. In 1873 their General Time Convention passed a vote commending his work. It did not adopt it.

In Canada, the railway engineer Sandford Fleming was pushing something even bigger: a single time system for the whole planet.

Ideas were not the problem. Power was. Congress had looked at national standard time in 1809, in 1870 and again in 1872, and turned it down each time. Washington would not act. So the railroads did.

> No government created American time zones. A trade group did.

## The man with the timetable

William Frederick Allen was the secretary of the General Time Convention and managing editor of the *Travelers' Official Guide to the Railways*, the national book of railroad timetables. If anyone understood the mess, it was the man who had to print it.

In 1881 the railway managers asked Allen to study the problem. He sent out questionnaires and gathered opinions. Then he drew lines on a map.

His plan replaced the railroads' roughly 50 times with five zones, each an hour apart: Intercolonial, Eastern, Central, Mountain and Pacific. The four main zones were centred on the 75th, 90th, 105th and 120th meridians west of Greenwich. One rule mattered most for winning people over: railway time would never be more than 30 minutes off anyone's local sun time.

On October 11, 1883, the General Time Convention met at the Grand Pacific Hotel in Chicago. Allen presented his plan. The railroad officials approved it. The switch was set for noon on Sunday, November 18.

## Two noons, one signal

To make a continent change its clocks on the same day, you need a referee. Two observatories agreed to play that role: the United States Naval Observatory in Washington and the Allegheny Observatory in Pittsburgh. Both agreed to send time signals down the telegraph wires.

When the day came, stations, banks and jewellers reset their clocks to the signal. In New York, the new Eastern time ran four minutes behind the city's old local time. In several cities, the clock struck noon twice: once by the sun, once by the railroad.

## Follow the power

Ask who won and who lost, and the story sharpens.

The railroads won. Fifty times became five, their timetables became simpler, and the whole country's clock now ran to their design.

Ordinary towns lost something harder to name. Their noon no longer belonged to their own sky. On November 17, the *Indianapolis Sentinel* complained that "the sun is no longer to boss the job." The paper grumbled that people would now have to eat, sleep, marry and even die by railroad time.

And the federal government? It tried to say no. Days before the switch, Attorney General Benjamin Brewster ruled that government clocks could not leave local time without the approval of Congress. The Naval Observatory, the nation's official timekeeper, sent out railroad time to its subscribers anyway.

Most cities adopted the new time right away. Many more joined within a year. Some towns held out. But the system kept spreading, helped by a simple fact: anyone who wanted to catch a train already had to use it.

## Thirty-five years without a law

Here is the strangest part. For 35 years, the time that nearly all of America lived by was a private agreement between companies.

Congress finally caught up on March 19, 1918, with the Standard Time Act. The law wrote the railroad zones into federal law and handed the job of drawing zone boundaries to the Interstate Commerce Commission. The same act also created daylight saving time. That part proved so unpopular that Congress repealed it in 1919, overriding a presidential veto to do it. Standard time stayed.

So the next time your phone quietly jumps an hour when you cross a zone line, remember where that line came from. Not a king, not a parliament, not a treaty. A timetable editor, a hotel ballroom in Chicago, and a vote by people who needed their trains to agree on what time it was.

The rules we treat as laws of nature were often written by someone who needed them first. That means they can be rewritten too.

## Follow The Breadcrumbs

Read **Ian R. Bartky, *Selling the True Time: Nineteenth-Century Timekeeping in America*** (Stanford University Press, 2000). It is a full-length scholarly history of how nineteenth-century Americans measured, sold and finally standardized time, and it puts the Chicago vote in its wider story. Look for it through your local library.
