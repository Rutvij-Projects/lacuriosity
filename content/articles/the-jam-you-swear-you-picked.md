Sometime today, someone will ask why you did something, and you will answer right away. Why that sandwich. Why that route. Why you said no to that invitation. Your answer will feel like a report from inside your own head.

It may be closer to a press release, written after the fact by a part of you that wasn't in the room.

I don't enjoy that idea. When I went looking for the evidence, I expected a clever lab curiosity that fell apart in real life. What I found surprised me, because the most convincing version of it happened next to the drinks aisle of a Swedish supermarket, with a jar of jam.

## The jar with two tops

Picture a tasting stand in a supermarket in Lund, Sweden. Two friendly researchers, presenting themselves as independent consultants, invite passing shoppers to try two jams and say which they prefer. In all, 180 people took part, aged 16 to 80.

The jars were a magic trick. Each one was really two small containers glued bottom to bottom, with a lid at each end and a paper wrapper hiding the join. It looked like one ordinary jar holding one ordinary jam, but it held a different jam at each end.

While a shopper tasted, the experimenter quietly turned the jar upside down. So when the shopper pointed at a favorite and was offered one more spoonful, the spoon went into the jam they had just turned down. Then came the simple question: why did you like this one better?

The pairs weren't subtle. One was spicy Cinnamon-Apple against bitter Grapefruit. The same test with tea pitted sweet Mango against Pernod, which smells of liquorice and aniseed.

Across all the jam trials, only 14.4% of the swaps were caught on the spot. Even after counting people who mentioned something odd later, or who remarked that the taste had changed without realizing why, the total reached just 33.3%. Tea came in at 32.2%.

So in about two out of three swaps, a person tasted the thing they had rejected and carried on explaining why it was their favorite.

> We don't only explain our choices. Sometimes we explain choices we never made.

Here is the detail that stays with me. The shoppers weren't struggling to tell the flavors apart. They rated the jams as easy to distinguish, 7.3 out of 10, and they felt just as confident on the swapped trials they missed as on the honest ones.

## It started with a photograph

The jam study grew out of an earlier experiment by Petter Johansson, Lars Hall and their colleagues, published in Science in 2005. They showed 120 people pairs of female faces and asked which was more attractive. Then, using a card magician's sleight of hand, they sometimes slid across the photo of the face the person had rejected and asked them to explain their choice.

No more than 26% of those swaps were detected. The researchers gave the effect a name: choice blindness.

The explanations were the eerie part. People often praised features that belonged only to the face in their hand, the one they had not picked. When independent raters compared explanations for real and swapped choices, they found no difference in emotion, detail or confidence. A made-up reason sounded exactly like a real one.

Faces and jam are low stakes, though. Surely we know what we believe about things that matter?

## Then they tried it on morals and votes

In 2012 the same group approached 160 people walking through a park and handed them a survey on a clipboard. It asked for opinions on moral questions, such as whether governments should be allowed to monitor email and internet traffic to fight terrorism.

A small slip of paper was lightly glued over part of the first page. When a person folded that page back, the slip stuck to the back of the clipboard and revealed a reworded statement underneath. Their rating stayed the same, but the sentence now said the opposite. "Permitted" could become "forbidden."

Then people read their answers aloud and explained them. A full 69% accepted at least one reversed statement as their own. And 53% argued clearly, in at least one case, for the opposite of what they had said minutes earlier.

In 2013 the team took the trick into the final stretch of Sweden's 2010 general election, recruiting voters in Malmö and Lund. They altered survey answers to place people nearer the opposing political camp. No more than 22% of the changed answers were corrected, and 92% of participants accepted the altered overall result. Afterward, 48% said they were open to considering a shift between coalitions, at a time when polls suggested no more than about 10% of voters were up for grabs.

That number deserves care. The study measured what people said they might do, not the ballots they cast. Most of this research also comes from one group of scientists, and it doesn't show that people have no real preferences. Detection did rise when the two options were the most different. A third of the jam swaps were noticed, too. Our sense of what we want is not fiction. It is just far looser than it feels from the inside.

## The storyteller is also a gift

It would be easy to read all this as an insult. I've come to read it differently.

If part of the mind is always building a story about why we act, then that story is not carved in stone. Those people in the park weren't stupid or dishonest. They were doing what we all do, making sense of themselves with the evidence in front of them. Perhaps that is why people can change. You can look at an old opinion, find you can argue the other side, and discover you were never as locked in as you believed.

## Brain Hack: write it down before you explain it

Next time you make a choice that matters to you, try a two-line note before anyone asks you about it.

- **Line one:** what you chose.
- **Line two:** the real reason, in one plain sentence, written right then.

Check it a week later, when you are telling the story of why you did it. If today's reason doesn't match the note, you have just caught your inner storyteller at work. This is a small habit inspired by the research rather than a tested cure, but it gives you something the shoppers didn't have: a record your memory can't quietly flip.

Then ask the most freeing question I know: if I could argue the other side this easily, what do I actually want?
