Hit a lump of pitch with a hammer and it shatters like glass. Sharp edges, flying chips, the whole thing. By every test you could run in a kitchen, it is a solid.

It is not. It is a liquid. And for almost a century, a funnel in Brisbane, Australia, has been proving it, one drop at a time, so slowly that no human being has ever seen a drop fall.

## A lesson that would not end

In 1927, Thomas Parnell, the first professor of physics at the University of Queensland, wanted to show his students something strange. Some things that look solid are really liquids that move unbelievably slowly.

So he heated a batch of pitch, the black, tar-like stuff once used to waterproof ships, and poured it into a glass funnel with a sealed stem. Then he waited three years for it to settle.

In 1930, the seal was cut. The pitch was free to flow.

It took eight years for the first drop to fall, in December 1938. Nobody saw it happen. That would become a habit.

> A solid is not a kind of stuff. It is a question about how long you are willing to wait.

## Where the rule breaks

Here is the assumption we all carry: solids keep their shape, liquids flow. Ice is solid, water is liquid, done.

Physicists stress-test that rule with one number, called viscosity. It measures how much a fluid resists flowing. Water has low viscosity. Honey has more. Cold honey, more still.

From the speed of the drops, the experimenters estimated pitch is about 100 billion times more viscous than water, according to the University of Queensland's own physics museum. A later estimate, made after the drop in 2000, pushed that to around 230 billion. The two figures disagree, but both say the same thing. Pitch flows. It just flows on a timescale longer than a school year, longer than a career.

That is why the hammer fools you. A hammer blow lasts a fraction of a second. In that instant, pitch has no time to flow, so it cracks. Give it a decade, and the same stuff stretches into a drop.

Here is a kitchen version. Pour honey from a spoon and it runs. Now picture honey a hundred billion times thicker. Turn the jar upside down at your birth, and you might see one drip by your eighth birthday.

## The drops nobody saw

The record reads like a slow heartbeat:

- 1938, 1947, 1954, 1962, 1970, 1979: one drop roughly every eight years
- July 1988: the seventh
- November 2000: the eighth
- April 2014: the ninth

And here is the cruel part. Every single drop fell unwatched.

In 1961, a physicist named John Mainstone took over as custodian. He looked after the funnel for 52 years. He came close. The seventh drop fell in July 1988, during World Expo 88 in Brisbane. Mainstone had stepped away for a drink. He missed it.

For the eighth drop, in November 2000, a webcam was trained on the funnel. Technical problems meant the camera recorded nothing at the moment it mattered.

Mainstone died in August 2013, aged 78, still waiting. The ninth drop fell the following April, and even that was strange. It had stretched down until it touched the pile of old drops below. While the new custodian, Andrew White, was swapping the beaker underneath, the wooden base wobbled and the drop snapped free.

## Why the drops slowed down

Look at the dates again. The early gaps were about eight years. The last two were over twelve and thirteen.

What changed? Air conditioning. It was installed in the building some time after the seventh drop. Cooler pitch is thicker pitch, so each drop now hangs and stretches longer before it lets go.

That is the experiment teaching a second lesson nobody planned. Viscosity is not fixed. Temperature rules it. Warm the room a few degrees and the tar speeds up. Cool it, and the clock slows down.

## Caught on camera, on the other side of the world

The Brisbane funnel is not alone. In October 1944, a colleague of the Nobel-winning physicist Ernest Walton set up a similar funnel at Trinity College Dublin. Then it sat, mostly forgotten, on a shelf.

In April 2013, physicists there noticed a drop forming. They moved the funnel to a table and pointed a webcam at it. At around 5 p.m. on 11 July 2013, the drop fell, and the camera caught it. It was the first pitch drop ever recorded on video. The Dublin team estimated their pitch was around 20 billion times more viscous than water.

So humans have now seen it happen, on a screen. In Brisbane, no one has seen it live.

## Still dripping

Parnell's funnel is still running, in a cabinet in the University of Queensland's Parnell Building. Guinness World Records lists it as the world's longest continuously running laboratory experiment. In 2005, Parnell and Mainstone shared an Ig Nobel Prize in physics, the award for research that first makes people laugh and then makes them think.

The tenth drop is forming right now. A live stream points at it, and hundreds of thousands of people check in each year. There is enough pitch left in the funnel, by Wikipedia's account, for at least another hundred years.

Somewhere in that century, a drop will fall. Maybe this time someone will be looking.

## Try This At Home

You can build your own "solid liquid" in two minutes. Mix about half a cup of cornflour (cornstarch) with roughly a third of a cup of water in a bowl, stirring slowly until it feels like thick cream.

Now punch it, fast. It pushes back like a solid. Then rest your fingers on top gently, and they sink right in. Scoop some up and roll it quickly into a ball, then stop. It melts through your fingers.

This mixture is not pitch. It works the opposite way: it stiffens when you push hard, while pitch shatters when you hit it fast. But both teach the same surprise. Whether something acts solid or liquid depends on how fast you ask the question.

Then do the thought experiment. If you set up a funnel today, how old would you be when the third drop fell?
