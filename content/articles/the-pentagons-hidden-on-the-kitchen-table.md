What becomes possible if everyone understood that "finished" is only a claim, and claims can be checked by anyone?

Consider a kitchen in San Diego in the mid-1970s. A countertop, a pencil, a few sheets of paper covered in odd shapes. The children are at school. The woman at the counter is drawing five-sided figures, over and over, adjusting an angle here, a side there. When the front door opens, the papers disappear into a drawer. Her daughter, years later, would say she thought her mother was just doodling.

She was not doodling. She was doing original mathematics, and she was about to correct the experts.

## The floor problem

Start with a question a tiler might ask. Which shapes can cover a floor with no gaps and no overlaps, using copies of just one shape?

Squares work. So do equilateral triangles and regular hexagons, which is why honeycombs look the way they do. Every triangle works, and so does every four-sided shape, however lopsided. But the regular pentagon, the neat one on a school poster, does not. Lay a few of them around a point and you get a sliver of empty floor that nothing can fill.

That leaves a stranger question. Some pentagons, stretched and skewed in just the right ways, do tile a floor. Which ones? How many different families of them exist?

Mathematicians had been chipping at this since 1918, when Karl Reinhardt found five types. Decades later, Richard Kershner found three more. In a 1968 proof, he claimed the list was now complete: eight types, no more.

> The list was declared finished. It was wrong, and the people who showed it were not professionals.

## A column, a son, a subscription

Martin Gardner wrote the "Mathematical Games" column in Scientific American, a monthly invitation for ordinary readers to play with real ideas. In July 1975 he wrote about tiling the plane with convex polygons, and he passed on Kershner's verdict that the pentagon question was closed.

Marjorie Rice read it. She had been born Marjorie Jeuck in Florida in 1923 and had grown up attending a one-room country school. She took only one year of maths in high school. College was never considered. She married Gilbert Rice in 1945, moved to San Diego, and raised five children. She had once worked as a commercial artist.

According to Quanta Magazine, she had given one of her sons a subscription to Scientific American partly so she could read it herself while the children were at school.

Within months, someone else had already poked a hole in the "complete" list. Richard James III, a software engineer, read the same column and found a new type of tiling pentagon. Gardner published it in his December 1975 column.

Perhaps that was the spark. If the list had one hole, it might have more.

## Hieroglyphics

Rice had no formal training in how to attack such a problem, so she built her own tools. In her own words, quoted by Quanta, she "developed my own notation system and in a few months discovered a new type."

Her notation was a private language for the conditions on a pentagon's sides and angles: which sides must be equal, which angles must add up to 360 degrees where tiles meet. By February 1976 she had found her first new type. By December 1977 she had found four in total, along with dozens upon dozens of different ways to arrange them. By one tally it was nearly sixty distinct tessellations.

She mailed her work to Gardner. Gardner sent it to Doris Schattschneider, a tiling expert at Moravian College in Pennsylvania. Schattschneider was sceptical at first; Rice's notation looked to her like hieroglyphics. Then she worked through it carefully and found that it was right. Every pentagon checked out.

Schattschneider wrote up the discoveries in Mathematics Magazine in 1978 and spent years making sure Rice received credit. Gardner later called her work "fantastic achievements" in a 1988 collection of his columns.

## Patterns with a painter's eye

There is a reason an artist might see what specialists missed. A tiling is, before it is anything else, a picture. Rice could look at a pentagon and imagine its neighbours turning and flipping around it, the way a quilter sees a whole quilt in a single block.

She never stopped seeing them as art. She turned her pentagon grids into Escher-like designs of flowers, shells, butterflies and bees, and wrote a chapter on the method for a 2003 book about M. C. Escher's legacy. Her daughter Kathy later said that when she looked through her mother's files, she found octagons and hexagons too. The curiosity kept going after the famous discoveries were done.

Notice the quiet historical rhyme. Escher himself had no mathematics degree, yet professional mathematicians studied his prints for their symmetry. Rice took the road in the other direction: an amateur who started from mathematics and ended in art.

## The list, finally closed

After Rice's four, the count stood at thirteen. Rolf Stein found a fourteenth type in 1985. A fifteenth turned up in 2015, when Casey Mann, Jennifer McLoud-Mann and David Von Derau at the University of Washington Bothell had a computer search through a huge but finite set of possibilities.

Then, in 2017, the French mathematician Michaël Rao announced a computer-assisted proof that there are exactly fifteen families of convex pentagons that tile the plane, and no others. Rice's four are among them. The mathematician Thomas Hales independently checked part of the work that same year, though full peer review took longer.

Marjorie Rice died in San Diego on 2 July 2017, aged 94. Dementia meant she was unable to learn that the problem she had helped crack open had, at last, been closed.

Her pentagons outlived the question. In 1999, one of her tilings became the floor of the foyer at the headquarters of the Mathematical Association of America in Washington, D.C. Every visitor walks across her kitchen-table discovery on the way in.

Consider what that floor quietly says. A proof can be wrong. An expert can be outdone by a reader. And the only tools required to check were curiosity, patience and a pencil.

## Look Closer

This week, spend twenty minutes with Quanta Magazine's article "Marjorie Rice's Secret Pentagons" (July 2017), free online at quantamagazine.org. It includes images of her tilings. Then take a sheet of paper, draw any pentagon you like, cut out six copies, and try to fit them together without gaps. You will feel, in your fingers, exactly how hard her problem was.
