Start with the ocean floor, 130 metres down, in the cold North Atlantic. Beneath it, the plates that make up Iceland are slowly pulling apart. On the sea floor, rock is molten, water is crushing, and for a while the two fight to a standstill. The pressure of all that water keeps explosions quiet.

Then the pile of new rock climbs toward the surface, and the water above it thins. On 14 November 1963, the cook on the trawler Ísleifur II spotted a column of dark smoke rising southwest of the boat. The pressure had lost the argument. A volcano had just broken the surface off Iceland's south coast.

Now zoom in on what happened next. The eruption kept going until 5 June 1967. By the end, it had built an island of about 2.7 square kilometres, though one 1969 Icelandic account gives 2.8, so the exact figure depends on who measured. It was named Surtsey, after Surtr, a fire giant of Norse myth.

## A blank slate, on purpose

Here is the rare part. Iceland protected the island from the day it was born. Nobody was allowed to build, farm, or plant. Visits needed a permit, and casual sightseeing stops are still prohibited.

That choice turned Surtsey into something almost nothing else on Earth is: a place where scientists could watch life arrive from zero, with no human thumb on the scale. UNESCO listed it as a World Heritage Site in 2008, citing its value as a natural laboratory for how life colonises new land.

> Ecology usually starts in the middle of the story. Surtsey let us watch the first page being written.

## The sea rocket goes first

The first plant turned up on 3 June 1965, while the volcano was still erupting. It was a seedling of sea rocket, a salt-tolerant shoreline plant, washed up on the new black beach. Sources differ on the exact subspecies name, but all agree it came in from the sea.

Then, mostly, nothing worked. Early plants did not last: they were buried in sand or scoured away by winter surf. Through 1975 to 1985, very few new species arrived and the pace of change slowed. By 1986, about 23 vascular plant species had been recorded, and only a few coastal species were really thriving.

Lava is not soil. It has almost no nitrogen, and no plant can build a life without it. Seeds could arrive on the wind and the waves, but the ground had nothing to offer them.

## The birds bring the fertiliser

In 1986, a few pairs of lesser black-backed gulls began nesting on a lava plain at the southern end of the island. It was a tiny colony. Its effect was enormous.

Gulls eat fish at sea and drop it on land, as droppings, feathers and scraps. The result was nitrogen and carbon, delivered to rock that had neither. Researchers found that the nitrogen the gulls brought was the critical factor driving succession on the island. Once a dense gull colony formed, numerous new plant species followed over the next years.

The numbers are striking. By 2008, plant cover and biomass inside the gull colony were 10 to 40 times greater than on the barren ground outside it. One study found respiration inside the colony 59 times higher than outside it, at the same temperature. Life, it turns out, builds its own soil, and one bird species held the key.

By 2013, 69 vascular plant species had been recorded on Surtsey, 59 were still present, and 39 had established viable populations. Researchers put the 2008 tally of surviving species at about 16 percent of Iceland's native vascular flora. Not every trend goes up: one 2014 paper reports a net loss of species since 2007. Colonisation, it seems, is a churn rather than a staircase.

## The race against the waves

The sea that delivered the seeds is also taking the island back. Estimates differ. By one account erosion had already halved the island's area, with more loss predicted. By another, the area was about 1.3 square kilometres in 2012. The 2007 World Heritage nomination argued that the pace of erosion is slowing, and that the resistant core should last a long time.

Either way, the story has a shape worth sitting with. A volcano built the land. Waves are trimming it. Between those two forces, a few gulls and a handful of seeds started an ecosystem from scratch, and a few generations of researchers wrote it down.

## Coordinates

Surtsey sits at about 63.303° N, 20.605° W, off the south coast of Iceland in the Westman Islands, and it is the southernmost point of the country. You cannot simply land there: access needs a permit. But you can find it on any map, zoom in to a speck of green on a dark island, and know what each patch of green had to get through to exist.
